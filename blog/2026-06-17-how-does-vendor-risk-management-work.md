Businesses today rely on third-party vendors for cloud services, software, payment processing, IT support, and business operations. While these partnerships improve efficiency and reduce operational costs, they also introduce significant cybersecurity challenges. Understanding **how vendor risk management works** is essential for protecting sensitive business information, reducing cyber risk, and preventing costly security incidents.

The urgency is no longer theoretical. According to Verizon's **2026 Data Breach Investigations Report**, **48% of all breaches now involve a third party, a 60% jump in a single year**. IBM's **2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report** puts the global average breach at **$4.44 million**, while supply chain compromises cost even more at **$4.91 million** and take an average of **267 days** to detect and contain. A single vulnerable supplier can expose your organization to a data breach, regulatory penalties, and operational disruption.

In this guide, you'll learn how vendor risk management works, why it's important, and how businesses can strengthen their cybersecurity by managing vendor relationships effectively.

## What Is Vendor Risk Management?

Vendor risk management is the structured process of identifying, assessing, monitoring, and reducing the cybersecurity, operational, financial, and compliance risks introduced by third-party vendors.

Every organization works with external partners, including software providers, cloud platforms, payment processors, managed service providers, consultants, and suppliers. Since many of these vendors have access to sensitive information or critical systems, businesses must continuously evaluate their security posture to reduce third-party risk.

Unlike traditional vendor reviews that occur once a year, modern vendor risk management combines **continuous security monitoring, security ratings, and external attack surface management** to provide real-time visibility into vendor security.

## How Does Vendor Risk Management Work?

The process follows several continuous steps that help organizations identify vulnerabilities before attackers can exploit them.

### 1. Identify All Third-Party Vendors

The first step is building a complete inventory of every third-party vendor connected to your organization. These may include:

- Cloud service providers
- SaaS vendors
- IT support companies
- Payment processors
- Marketing agencies
- Software developers
- Supply chain partners

Each vendor is categorized according to the type of data they access and the level of cyber risk they introduce. Critical vendors that handle confidential information or support essential business operations receive additional oversight because they present a higher level of third-party risk.

### 2. Conduct a Vendor Security Assessment

After identifying vendors, organizations perform a comprehensive vendor security assessment that evaluates whether a vendor follows recognized cybersecurity standards and best practices. Typical assessment areas include:

- Information security policies
- Regulatory compliance
- Data encryption
- Access controls
- Identity management
- Incident response planning
- Employee security awareness
- Business continuity planning

Many organizations also distribute detailed security questionnaires to understand how vendors protect sensitive information. A thorough assessment helps identify weaknesses before establishing a business relationship.

### 3. Evaluate Third-Party Risk

Once assessment data has been collected, organizations analyze the vendor's overall third-party risk. Several factors influence risk levels, including:

- Access to confidential information
- Criticality of business services
- Compliance requirements
- Previous security incidents
- Existing security controls
- Geographic location
- Industry regulations

Understanding third-party risk enables organizations to prioritize remediation efforts and allocate resources where they're needed most.

### 4. Monitor Vendors with Continuous Security Monitoring

Cyber threats evolve daily, making annual assessments insufficient, especially now that the majority of breaches touch a third party. Continuous security monitoring provides ongoing visibility into a vendor's cybersecurity posture, delivering near real-time insights instead of waiting months to discover a vulnerability. Continuous monitoring helps identify:

- Newly discovered vulnerabilities
- Exposed internet-facing assets
- Misconfigured systems
- Certificate issues
- Email security weaknesses
- Malware infections
- Credential exposures

With supply chain breaches taking **267 days** on average to detect and contain, early detection is the single biggest lever for reducing impact and cost.

### 5. Use Security Ratings to Measure Cyber Risk

Security ratings provide an objective measurement of a vendor's external cybersecurity posture. Unlike questionnaires that represent a point-in-time assessment, security ratings continuously evaluate publicly observable security signals, typically measuring:

- Patch management
- SSL certificate health
- DNS security
- Email authentication
- Vulnerability exposure
- Network security
- Malware detection

Organizations use security ratings to compare vendors, identify high-risk suppliers, and continuously monitor changes in their security posture, strengthening vendor risk management with actionable insights that don't require constant manual reviews.

### 6. Reduce Cyber Risk Through Remediation

The final stage focuses on reducing identified risks. Organizations collaborate with vendors to address security gaps through remediation plans. Common remediation activities include:

- Enabling multi-factor authentication
- Applying security patches
- Improving access management
- Updating security policies
- Strengthening endpoint protection
- Conducting employee cybersecurity training

Reducing cyber risk is not a one-time activity. It requires ongoing collaboration between organizations and their third-party vendors.

## Why Vendor Risk Management Matters

Cybercriminals increasingly target third-party vendors because they often have trusted access to larger organizations, and the data confirms the trend is accelerating. Among breaches with third-party involvement in the latest reporting, the large majority stem from system intrusion, where attackers exploit vendor relationships to reach their real target.

A single compromised vendor can lead to:

- Data breaches
- Financial losses
- Operational downtime
- Regulatory fines
- Reputational damage
- Supply chain disruptions

An effective vendor risk management program helps organizations reduce third-party risk, improve supply chain security, and strengthen overall cyber resilience, while supporting compliance with industry regulations and improving customer confidence in your security practices.

## Best Practices for Vendor Risk Management

Organizations should follow these best practices to improve vendor risk management:

- Maintain an up-to-date vendor inventory.
- Perform regular vendor security assessments.
- Implement continuous security monitoring.
- Monitor security ratings throughout the vendor lifecycle.
- Prioritize vendors based on third-party risk.
- Include cybersecurity requirements in vendor contracts.
- Reassess vendors after major business or technology changes.
- Incorporate external attack surface management to identify publicly exposed vendor assets.
- Develop incident response procedures for vendor-related security events.

These practices help organizations reduce cyber risk, improve supply chain security, and prevent data breaches before they occur.

## Final Thoughts

Understanding how vendor risk management works is no longer optional for organizations that depend on third-party vendors. With third-party involvement in breaches climbing to nearly half of all incidents, modern businesses must move beyond periodic assessments and adopt a proactive approach that combines vendor risk management, continuous security monitoring, security ratings, vendor security assessments, and external attack surface management.

By continuously identifying vulnerabilities and reducing third-party risk, organizations can strengthen their cybersecurity posture, improve supply chain security, reduce cyber risk, and protect sensitive business information from evolving threats.
